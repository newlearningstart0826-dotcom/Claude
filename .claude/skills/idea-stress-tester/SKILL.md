# Idea Stress Tester

A pre-writing skill that attacks a book idea the way the market will — before the author
invests months writing a book that fails at the idea level.

Most books don't fail because they're badly written. They fail because:
- The idea is too generic to stand out
- The market is saturated with near-identical books
- The author's angle isn't differentiated enough to justify another title
- The target reader is too broad to find or convert
- The core premise can't survive a skeptical editor's first question

This skill surfaces those problems *before* writing begins — when they're still cheap to fix.

The output has **five sections**, always delivered in full:

1. **Why This Book Might Fail** — honest failure mode analysis
2. **Market Saturation Analysis** — what already exists and how crowded the space is
3. **Competing Angles** — the strongest versions of similar books and how they'd beat this one
4. **Unique Positioning Suggestions** — the angles that would make this book genuinely defensible
5. **Brutal Rewrite** — a sharper, stronger version of the core idea

---

## Intake

Collect the idea before stress-testing. Keep this short — over-explaining the idea before
the test contaminates the result.

Ask only:

> "Tell me your book idea — describe it the way you would to someone at a dinner party. Two or
> three sentences. Don't sell it to me, just tell me what it is."

Then ask two follow-up questions:

> "Who is this for — specifically? And what's the one thing you most want readers to walk away
> believing or able to do?"

That's enough. Do not let the author pre-justify the idea. The stress test should work on
the idea as it currently exists, not the defended version of it.

If the author has already used **book-architect**, pull the core premise, target reader, and
unique angle from the Positioning Plan instead of re-asking.

## The Stress Test Report

### Section 1 — Why This Book Might Fail

The most important section. Deliver this with honesty and specificity — not to discourage,
but because vague encouragement leads to wasted years.

Analyse failure modes across **six dimensions**:

#### 1A — The Premise Problem
Is the core idea strong enough to carry a book?

Attack vectors:
- **Too broad**: Could this be reduced to a blog post or a chapter in someone else's book?
- **Too narrow**: Is the audience so specific that not enough people will care?
- **Too obvious**: Is the premise something most readers in this space already know or believe?
- **Too contrarian**: Is the argument so provocative it'll alienate the exact reader who needs it?
- **Unprovable**: Can the author actually demonstrate this claim, or is it ultimately just opinion?

```
PREMISE VERDICT: [Strong / Workable / Needs sharpening / Fundamental problem]
KEY RISK: [The single most dangerous weakness in the premise]
```

#### 1B — The Author Problem
Is this author the right person to write this book?

Attack vectors:
- **Credential gap**: Does the author have lived experience or demonstrated expertise, or is this
  a well-researched outsider's take on someone else's world?
- **Voice gap**: Is there evidence this author can sustain 60,000 words at the quality the
  premise demands?
- **Access gap**: Does the book require stories, data, or relationships the author may not have?
- **Timing gap**: Is the author 2 years too early (hasn't earned the platform) or 5 years too
  late (the window has passed)?

```
AUTHOR FIT VERDICT: [Strong fit / Workable / Needs bolstering / Significant gap]
KEY RISK: [The most important credential, access, or timing problem]
```

#### 1C — The Reader Problem
Is the target reader real, reachable, and ready to buy?

Attack vectors:
- **Too diffuse**: "Anyone who wants to improve their life" is not a target reader
- **Already served**: Does this reader have 5 other books that do what this one does?
- **Not buying books**: Some audiences have the problem but don't purchase non-fiction to solve it
- **Can't find them**: Even if they exist, is there a channel to reach them at scale?
- **Wrong format**: Would this reader prefer a course, a podcast, a newsletter — not a book?

```
READER VERDICT: [Specific & reachable / Workable / Too broad / Fundamental mismatch]
KEY RISK: [The most critical reader acquisition or relevance problem]
```

#### 1D — The Timing Problem
Is this book arriving at the right moment?

Attack vectors:
- **Too early**: The cultural conversation hasn't caught up — readers don't yet feel the problem
- **Too late**: The peak of the wave has passed; the category leaders are entrenched
- **Event-dependent**: The relevance depends on a trend that may not last the 2–3 years it
  takes to write, publish, and find an audience
- **Evergreen risk**: Conversely, is the idea so timeless it has no urgency — no reason to
  read it now rather than in five years?

```
TIMING VERDICT: [Ideal window / Good timing / Slightly late / Significant concern]
KEY RISK: [The timing threat most likely to affect the book's reception]
```

#### 1E — The Differentiation Problem
What stops a reader choosing an existing book over this one?

This is the question that kills more book ideas than any other. It's not enough to have a good
idea — the reader needs a reason to choose *this book* over the three they've already half-read
on their nightstand.

Attack vectors:
- **The "why not just read X?" problem**: Name the most obvious comparable book — would a
  rational reader choose this one instead? Why?
- **The summary problem**: Can the book's core value be adequately conveyed in a 10-minute
  summary, making the full read unnecessary?
- **The authority problem**: Is there a more credible author covering this ground?

```
DIFFERENTIATION VERDICT: [Clearly distinct / Marginal difference / Undifferentiated / Redundant]
KEY RISK: [The existing book or resource that most directly competes with this idea]
```

#### 1F — The Execution Problem
Even if the idea is sound, can it be executed well enough to matter?

Attack vectors:
- **Evidence thin**: Does the book require data, access, or case studies the author may not get?
- **Scope creep**: Is the idea too ambitious to execute well in one book?
- **Voice mismatch**: Does the premise demand a writing style the author hasn't demonstrated?
- **Single-idea risk**: Can this sustain a full book, or is it a 3,000-word essay stretched?

```
EXECUTION VERDICT: [Fully executable / Manageable / Challenging / High risk]
KEY RISK: [The execution challenge most likely to undermine the final book]
```

### Section 2 — Market Saturation Analysis

Research what already exists in this space and assess how crowded it is.

#### What to assess

**The existing category leaders**
Identify the 3–5 books that most directly compete with this idea. For each:
- What angle do they take?
- What's their target reader?
- How successful are they? (bestseller / mid-list / cult following / faded)
- What do they do well that this book would need to match or beat?
- What do they conspicuously *not* do that leaves a gap?

**The category shape**
- Is this a growing category (more books selling well each year) or a declining one?
- Is the category dominated by one or two breakout titles that defined the conversation?
- Is there a recent entrant that already took the fresh angle the author was considering?

**The gap map**
After mapping what exists, identify: what *isn't* there? What angle, audience, or approach
has the existing books left uncovered?

```
MARKET SATURATION REPORT
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

CATEGORY:        [Name of the book category / shelf position]
SATURATION LEVEL:[Uncrowded / Competitive / Saturated / Dominated]

CATEGORY LEADERS:
  ① [Title] by [Author] — [Angle / audience / what it does well / its gap]
  ② [Title] by [Author] — [...]
  ③ [Title] by [Author] — [...]

RECENT ENTRANTS (last 2–3 years):
  • [Title] — [How it approached the space and how it performed]
  • [Title] — [...]

THE GAP MAP:
  COVERED:      [What the existing books collectively address well]
  UNCOVERED:    [What they leave untouched — audiences, angles, depths]
  THE OPENING:  [Where a new book could find uncontested ground]

MARKET VERDICT:
  [Is there room for this book? Under what conditions? What would need
   to be true for this book to win space in an already-occupied category?]
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
```

### Section 3 — Competing Angles

This section steelmans the competition. Rather than dismissing existing books, it argues
the *strongest possible case* for why a reader would choose them over the author's book —
then uses that to clarify what this book must do differently.

For each of the top 2–3 competitors, build the case against the author's book:

```
COMPETING ANGLE ANALYSIS
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

COMPETITOR 1: [Title] by [Author]
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
THEIR ANGLE:       [What they argue / how they frame the problem]
THEIR STRENGTH:    [Why a rational reader might choose them over this book]
THE READER THEY WIN: [Who they're better for, and why]
THEIR WEAKNESS:    [What they don't do — the gap they leave]
THE THREAT LEVEL:  [How directly they compete: Direct / Adjacent / Indirect]

IF A SMART EDITOR READ BOTH, THEY'D SAY:
"[A brutally honest comparative judgment — why the competitor currently
  wins the 'which should I read?' decision, and what this author would
  need to do to change that verdict]"
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

COMPETITOR 2: [Title] by [Author]
[Same structure]

COMPETITOR 3: [Title] by [Author]
[Same structure]

COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE VERDICT:
  The book this author should be most afraid of: [Title — and why]
  The competitor that leaves the biggest opening: [Title — and what gap to exploit]
  What this book must do that none of the competitors do: [Specific]
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
```

### Section 4 — Unique Positioning Suggestions

Having exposed the problems and the competitive landscape, this section proposes 4–5
concrete alternative angles the author could take to create a genuinely defensible book.

Each suggestion is a *repositioning* — same general territory, but a different entry point,
audience, argument, or format that would give the book a real reason to exist.

```
UNIQUE POSITIONING OPTIONS
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

CURRENT POSITIONING:
  "[The author's idea as stated — summarised in one sentence]"

OPTION 1 — [Positioning Name]
  THE PIVOT:      [What specifically changes about the idea]
  NEW PREMISE:    "[Restated in one sentence]"
  TARGET READER:  [Who this now speaks to — more specifically]
  WHY IT WINS:    [What this angle has that existing books don't]
  THE RISK:       [What this approach would sacrifice or make harder]
  DIFFICULTY:     [Easy pivot / Medium / Requires significant rethinking]

OPTION 2 — [Positioning Name]
  [Same structure]

OPTION 3 — [Positioning Name: The Audience Reframe]
  [Keeps the core idea, but targets a completely different, more specific reader]

OPTION 4 — [Positioning Name: The Contrarian Angle]
  [Takes the same territory but argues the opposite of the category consensus]

OPTION 5 — [Positioning Name: The Format Shift]
  [Considers whether this idea is better as a manifesto, a field guide,
   a memoir with ideas, a case-study collection, or another form]

RECOMMENDED DIRECTION:
  [Of these 5, which has the strongest combination of differentiation,
   author fit, and market opportunity — and why]
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
```

### Section 5 — Brutal Rewrite

The final and most actionable section. Take the author's original idea and rewrite it —
sharper, more specific, more differentiated, and more defensible.

This isn't a cosmetic polish. It's a substantive rethinking that applies every lesson
from Sections 1–4: fixes the premise weakness, sharpens the audience, closes the
differentiation gap, and gives the book a clear reason to exist.

```
╔══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╗
║                    THE BRUTAL REWRITE                        ║
╚══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╝

ORIGINAL IDEA (as stated):
  "[The author's idea, verbatim or closely paraphrased]"

THE PROBLEMS WITH IT (in one sentence each):
  • [Problem 1]
  • [Problem 2]
  • [Problem 3]

THE REWRITTEN IDEA:
  "[The sharper version — more specific audience, clearer premise,
    differentiated angle, defensible position]"

WHAT CHANGED AND WHY:

  AUDIENCE:     [Original → Rewritten] — [Why the new audience is stronger]

  PREMISE:      [Original → Rewritten] — [Why the new premise is more defensible]

  ANGLE:        [Original → Rewritten] — [Why the new angle creates differentiation]

  PROMISE:      [Original → Rewritten] — [Why the new promise is more compelling
                                          and believable]

THE ONE-LINE PITCH (new version):
  "[A single sentence that would make a smart editor say 'I want to see that']"

WHAT THIS REWRITE DEMANDS FROM THE AUTHOR:
  [Be honest: what does the stronger version require that the original didn't?
   More access? Different credentials? A harder argument to prove?
   Name it — the author needs to know what they're signing up for.]

THE QUESTION THIS REWRITE MUST ANSWER BEFORE WRITING BEGINS:
  "[The single most important thing the author needs to verify or decide
   before this idea is ready to become a book proposal]"
╚══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╝
```

## After the Stress Test

Once all five sections are delivered, close with a clear handoff:

```
NEXT STEP RECOMMENDATION
─────────────────────────────────────────────────
Based on this stress test, the author should:

[ ] PROCEED with the original idea → feed into book-architect
    (idea is strong enough — stress test revealed no fundamental problems)

[ ] PROCEED with the rewritten idea → feed into book-architect
    (the brutal rewrite addresses the core weaknesses — use that version)

[ ] PAUSE and address [specific gap] before proceeding
    (one fixable problem must be solved first — brief on what that is)

[ ] RETHINK the idea more substantially before book-architect
    (fundamental problems in premise, differentiation, or author fit
     that repositioning alone won't fix)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

If the recommendation is to proceed, offer:
> "Want to take the rewritten idea straight into book-architect, or would you like to
> iterate on the positioning further first?"

## Tone & Honesty Standards

This skill must be genuinely critical — not performatively harsh, but not reassuring either.
The author is about to invest months or years. They deserve the truth.

**Be specific, not vague.** "The market is competitive" is useless. "Your idea sits directly
in the shadow of [specific title], which already owns your target reader and has 4,000 Amazon
reviews" is useful.

**Name the real risk.** If the biggest problem is that the author doesn't have the credentials
to make the book credible, say that. If the idea is fundamentally a blog post stretched thin,
say that. If the timing is genuinely bad, say that.

**But find the opening.** Honest stress-testing isn't demolition. Every idea has some angle
that's defensible. The job is to find it — and articulate clearly what it would take to get there.

**The test of a good stress test:** The author should finish this feeling genuinely informed
about what they're getting into — not deflated and not falsely encouraged. If they proceed,
they proceed knowing the real obstacles. If they pivot, they pivot with a better idea.

## Integration with Other Skills

**→ book-architect**: The stress test's output — specifically the Brutal Rewrite's repositioned
premise, sharpened audience, and differentiated angle — feeds directly into book-architect's
Positioning Plan. A stress-tested idea becomes a stronger architecture.

**→ research-evidence-builder**: Section 2 (Market Saturation) identifies the gap the book
must fill. That gap brief goes to research-evidence-builder to find supporting data, case
studies, and evidence that validates the opening.

**→ voice-calibrator**: If the stress test reveals an author fit problem (credential gap,
voice gap), run voice-calibrator before book-architect — to establish whether the author's
natural voice and authority level match the demand of the repositioned idea.
